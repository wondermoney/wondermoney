## Portfolio value

### What it means
The total value of the portfolio based on the current market value of its holdings.

### Where it appears
Portfolio summaries, performance views, and portfolio detail headers.

### How to read it
Compare it with your cost basis and with the same portfolio over time.

### What can make it change
Market price moves, new trades, dividends, fees, and currency effects.

### Common misunderstanding
It is not the same as cash invested or what you originally paid.

## Cash invested and cost basis

### What it means
Cash invested is the money you have put into the portfolio. Cost basis is the tracked amount you paid for the current holdings, including tracked costs.

### Where it appears
Portfolio totals, holding details, and gain/loss views.

### How to read it
Use it as the baseline for return and unrealized gain or loss.

### What can make it change
New buys, sells, transfers, and any tracked purchase costs.

### Common misunderstanding
Cost basis is not the same as current value.

## Cost basis method: FIFO and average cost

### What it means
The cost basis method decides which purchase cost a sale relieves. FIFO (first in, first out) sells the oldest lots first. Average cost relieves the running average of everything you still hold, and a sale leaves that average unchanged.

### Where it appears
In the portfolio settings, where you choose the method, and in the lots section of a holding's details, which lists each purchase lot with its date, quantity, remaining quantity, unit cost and fees.

### How to read it
Pick the method your tax rules use. Portugal and Spain use FIFO; Brazil and some other jurisdictions use average cost. Changing the method recalculates the portfolio's realized gains and rebuilds its daily history with the new cost basis.

### Example
Buy 10 units at 100 and 10 units at 200, then sell 10.

- FIFO relieves the first lot: realized cost 1,000, and the remaining 10 units keep an average cost of 200.
- Average cost relieves half of the 3,000 total: realized cost 1,500, and the remaining 10 units keep an average cost of 150.

### What can make it change
Changing the method in portfolio settings, new buys and sells, splits, fees, and transaction corrections.

### Common misunderstanding
The method changes cost basis and realized gains, not the market value of what you hold. A sale never changes the average cost per unit under the average method.

## Market value

### What it means
The current worth of a holding or portfolio based on the latest price.

### Where it appears
Portfolio cards, holdings tables, and allocation views.

### How to read it
Market value shows what the position is worth now, not what it cost.

### What can make it change
Price updates, changes in quantity, and currency movement.

### Common misunderstanding
Market value can move even when you make no trade.

## Unrealized gain or loss

### What it means
The profit or loss on positions you still hold.

### Where it appears
Performance summaries and holding rows.

### How to read it
Positive values mean the position is above cost basis; negative values mean it is below.

### What can make it change
Price changes, fees, and currency effects.

### Common misunderstanding
It only becomes real when you sell. Until then, it is unrealized.

## Realized gain or loss

### What it means
The profit or loss locked in when you sell or otherwise close a position. For each sale it is the proceeds after the sale's fee, less the cost basis the portfolio's method relieved (including the purchase fees of the units sold).

### Where it appears
- The portfolio summary, as a total for the portfolio or for all portfolios.
- The **Realized** column of the holdings table: everything that holding has realized so far.
- The **Realized gains** section, which lists every sale with its date, quantity, proceeds, cost basis, realized gain and the date of the oldest purchase it sold from (held since). Filter it by year and download the list shown as a CSV file.
- **Closed positions**: assets you have sold in full, with their lifetime result, so they stay visible after they leave the holdings.

### How to read it
Use it to see what has already been captured by completed trades. The sales of a year add up to that year's total, and every year together adds up to the portfolio's realized gains. A year is a calendar year in UTC, by the date of the sale.

### Example
Buy 10 units at 100 with a fee of 10, then sell 4 at 150 with a fee of 2. Under FIFO the sale relieves 4 × 100 plus 4 of the 10 purchase fee, a cost basis of 404. Its proceeds are 600 less the 2 fee, 598, so the sale realized 194.

### What can make it change
Sells, partial sells, fees, splits, transaction corrections and a change of cost basis method.

### Currency
Amounts are shown in your currency. A sale recorded in another currency is converted at the exchange rate of the day of the sale. A sale with no rate for that day is listed in its own currency and left out of the totals, with a note saying so.

### Common misunderstanding
Realized gain or loss is separate from unrealized gain or loss. WonderMoney reports what each sale made under your portfolio's cost basis method; it does not apply country-specific tax rules such as wash-sale rules, and it is not a tax form.

## Return percentage

### What it means
The percentage gain or loss relative to cost basis or invested amount.

### Where it appears
Performance charts, holding rows, and benchmark comparison views.

### How to read it
Compare return across time ranges only when the same method is used.

### What can make it change
Price moves, dividends, fees, and currency effects.

### Common misunderstanding
Return percentage is not the same as portfolio value.

## Time-weighted and money-weighted return

### What it means
Two returns for the selected period answer two different questions.

- **Time-weighted return** says how the investments did. Each day's return is measured net of the money put in or taken out that day, and the days are chained together, so a deposit or a withdrawal does not move it. Dividends paid out count as return.
- **Money-weighted return** says how your money did. It is the rate at which the value at the start of the period, every buy, sale and dividend in it, and the value at the end balance out: the same calculation as a spreadsheet's `XIRR`. Money that stayed invested longer weighs more.

### Where it appears
The portfolio summary, for one portfolio or for all of them together, for every period you can pick; and the portfolio performance report and its CSV download.

### How to read it
- When the two are close, the timing of your deposits and withdrawals made little difference.
- When the money-weighted return is higher, more of your money was invested while prices rose; when it is lower, more was invested while they fell.
- For a period of a year or more, the money-weighted return is a yearly rate. For a shorter period it is the rate over that period, because stretching a few weeks into a year would exaggerate it.
- With no deposits or withdrawals in the period, the time-weighted return is the change in value over the starting value.

### Example
You hold 10,000 at the start of the year. Prices stay flat until you add another 10,000 in June; then they rise 10%, and the portfolio ends the year at 22,000.

- Time-weighted return: +10%. The investments rose 10%; the deposit is not a gain.
- Money-weighted return: more than +10%, because twice the money was invested during the rise.

### Missing days
Both returns are measured from the portfolio's daily history. A day with no recorded value (for example while the history is rebuilt, or when no exchange rate converts it into your currency) is never read as zero: the last value is carried over it, and the summary says how many days were missing. Days when the portfolio held nothing, before its first buy or after everything was sold, are not missing.

### What can make it change
Price moves, dividends, fees, currency effects and the selected period. A buy or a sale changes the money-weighted return; it changes the time-weighted return only through what the money does afterwards.

### Common misunderstanding
Neither figure is the gain over cost basis. Compare a benchmark with the time-weighted return, because a benchmark has no deposits or withdrawals either.

## Dividends and income

### What it means
Cash income paid by investments, usually from dividends or similar distributions.

### Where it appears
Transaction lists, income summaries, and performance views.

### How to read it
Treat it as part of total return when you want the full picture.

### What can make it change
New dividend payments, reinvestments, withholding taxes, and corrections.

### Common misunderstanding
Income is not the same as profit on price movement.

## Allocation and holding weight

### What it means
Allocation shows how the portfolio is spread across assets. Holding weight shows how much one holding contributes to the whole.

### Where it appears
Allocation charts, holding tables, and portfolio summaries.

### How to read it
Look for concentration, balance, and whether one holding dominates the portfolio.

### What can make it change
New trades, price changes, dividends, and movements in cash balances.

### Common misunderstanding
Allocation describes structure, not a recommendation.

## Benchmark comparison

### What it means
A benchmark is a reference used to compare portfolio performance.

### Where it appears
Performance benchmark views and return charts.

### How to read it
Compare the portfolio and benchmark over the same time range and with the same currency context.

### What can make it change
Portfolio returns, benchmark price moves, and the selected time range.

### Common misunderstanding
A benchmark is a comparison line, not a suggestion to copy.

## Currency effects

### What it means
The part of the result caused by exchange-rate movement when holdings use another currency.

### Where it appears
Performance and value views for multi-currency holdings.

### How to read it
Separate asset performance from currency movement when you want to understand the source of change.

### What can make it change
Exchange-rate changes and foreign-currency holdings.

### Common misunderstanding
Currency effect is not the same as the asset’s own price move.

## Fees

### What it means
Tracked costs paid to trade, hold, or manage investments.

### Where it appears
Transaction views, return summaries, and realized results.

### How to read it
Fees reduce your net result, so review them alongside gains and income.

### What can make it change
New transactions, broker charges, and corrected imports.

### Common misunderstanding
Fees are part of performance, not a separate topic.

## Time range

### What it means
The period used to calculate or display performance and comparison data.

### Where it appears
Performance charts, benchmark views, and summary cards.

### How to read it
Short ranges can be noisy; longer ranges often show the underlying trend more clearly.

### What can make it change
Changing the selected period or moving the chart window.

### Common misunderstanding
Different time ranges can produce very different return figures.
