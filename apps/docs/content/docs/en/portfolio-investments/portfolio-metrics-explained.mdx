WonderMoney shows portfolio data so you can understand what changed. This page and its data are informational only. WonderMoney does not tell you what to buy, sell, or hold.

## Portfolio value

### What it means
The total value of the portfolio based on the current market value of its holdings.

### Where it appears
Portfolio summaries, performance views, and portfolio detail headers.

### How to read it
Compare it with your cost basis and with the same portfolio over time.

### What can make it change
Market price moves, new trades, dividends, fees, and currency effects.

### Common misunderstanding
It is not the same as cash invested or what you originally paid.

## Cash invested and cost basis

### What it means
Cash invested is the money you have put into the portfolio. Cost basis is the tracked amount you paid for the current holdings, including tracked costs.

### Where it appears
Portfolio totals, holding details, and gain/loss views.

### How to read it
Use it as the baseline for return and unrealized gain or loss.

### What can make it change
New buys, sells, transfers, and any tracked purchase costs.

### Common misunderstanding
Cost basis is not the same as current value.

## Cost basis method: FIFO and average cost

### What it means
The cost basis method decides which purchase cost a sale relieves. FIFO (first in, first out) sells the oldest lots first. Average cost relieves the running average of everything you still hold, and a sale leaves that average unchanged.

### Where it appears
In the portfolio settings, where you choose the method, and in the lots section of a holding's details, which lists each purchase lot with its date, quantity, remaining quantity, unit cost and fees.

### How to read it
The portfolio settings on the web offer two methods: **FIFO (oldest lots first)** and **Average cost**. A new portfolio starts on FIFO; the method is chosen in the settings, not when the portfolio is created. The hint under each option names countries where it is used, but WonderMoney does not check which method your tax rules require. Changing the method recalculates the portfolio's realized gains and rebuilds its daily history with the new cost basis.

### Example
Buy 10 units at 100 and 10 units at 200, then sell 10.

- FIFO relieves the first lot: realized cost 1,000, and the remaining 10 units keep an average cost of 200.
- Average cost relieves half of the 3,000 total: realized cost 1,500, and the remaining 10 units keep an average cost of 150.

### What can make it change
Changing the method in portfolio settings, new buys and sells, splits, fees, and transaction corrections.

### Common misunderstanding
The method changes cost basis and realized gains, not the market value of what you hold. A sale never changes the average cost per unit under the average method.

## Market value

### What it means
The current worth of a holding or portfolio based on the latest price.

### Where it appears
Portfolio cards, holdings tables, and allocation views.

### How to read it
Market value shows what the position is worth now, not what it cost.

### What can make it change
Price updates, changes in quantity, and currency movement.

### Common misunderstanding
Market value can move even when you make no trade.

## Unrealized gain or loss

### What it means
The profit or loss on positions you still hold.

### Where it appears
Performance summaries and holding rows.

### How to read it
Positive values mean the position is above cost basis; negative values mean it is below.

### What can make it change
Price changes, fees, and currency effects.

### Common misunderstanding
It only becomes real when you sell. Until then, it is unrealized.

## Realized gain or loss

### What it means
The profit or loss locked in when you sell or otherwise close a position. For each sale it is the proceeds after the sale's fee, less the cost basis the portfolio's method relieved (including the purchase fees of the units sold).

### Where it appears
- The portfolio summary, as a total for the portfolio or for all portfolios.
- The **Realized** column of the holdings table: everything that holding has realized so far.
- The **Realized gains** section, which lists every sale with its date, quantity, proceeds, cost basis, realized gain and the date of the oldest purchase it sold from (held since). Filter it by year and download the list shown as a CSV file.
- **Closed positions**: assets you have sold in full, with their lifetime result, so they stay visible after they leave the holdings.

### How to read it
Use it to see what has already been captured by completed trades. The sales of a year add up to that year's total, and every year together adds up to the portfolio's realized gains. A year is a calendar year in UTC, by the date of the sale.

### Example
Buy 10 units at 100 with a fee of 10, then sell 4 at 150 with a fee of 2. Under FIFO the sale relieves 4 × 100 plus 4 of the 10 purchase fee, a cost basis of 404. Its proceeds are 600 less the 2 fee, 598, so the sale realized 194.

### What can make it change
Sells, partial sells, fees, splits, transaction corrections and a change of cost basis method.

### Currency
Amounts are shown in your currency. A sale recorded in another currency is converted at the exchange rate of the day of the sale. A sale with no rate for that day is listed in its own currency and left out of the totals, with a note saying so.

### Common misunderstanding
Realized gain or loss is separate from unrealized gain or loss. WonderMoney reports what each sale made under your portfolio's cost basis method; it does not apply country-specific tax rules such as wash-sale rules, and it is not a tax form.

## Return percentage

### What it means
The percentage gain or loss relative to cost basis or invested amount.

### Where it appears
Performance charts, holding rows, and benchmark comparison views.

### How to read it
Compare return across time ranges only when the same method is used.

### What can make it change
Price moves, dividends, fees, and currency effects.

### Common misunderstanding
Return percentage is not the same as portfolio value.

## Time-weighted and money-weighted return

### What it means
Two returns for the selected period answer two different questions.

- **Time-weighted return** says how the investments did. Each day's return is measured net of the money put in or taken out that day, and the days are chained together, so a deposit or a withdrawal does not move it. Dividends paid out count as return.
- **Money-weighted return** says how your money did. It is the rate at which the value at the start of the period, every buy, sale and dividend in it, and the value at the end balance out: the same calculation as a spreadsheet's `XIRR`. Money that stayed invested longer weighs more.

### Where it appears
The portfolio summary, for one portfolio or for all of them together, for every period you can pick; the portfolio performance report and its CSV download; and, for the time-weighted return, the Benchmark Comparison chart, day by day.

### How to read it
- When the two are close, the timing of your deposits and withdrawals made little difference.
- When the money-weighted return is higher, more of your money was invested while prices rose; when it is lower, more was invested while they fell.
- For a period of a year or more, the money-weighted return is a yearly rate. For a shorter period it is the rate over that period, because stretching a few weeks into a year would exaggerate it.
- With no deposits or withdrawals in the period, the time-weighted return is the change in value over the starting value.

### Example
You hold 10,000 at the start of the year. Prices stay flat until you add another 10,000 in June; then they rise 10%, and the portfolio ends the year at 22,000.

- Time-weighted return: +10%. The investments rose 10%; the deposit is not a gain.
- Money-weighted return: more than +10%, because twice the money was invested during the rise.

### Missing days
Both returns are measured from the portfolio's daily history. A day with no recorded value (for example while the history is rebuilt, or when no exchange rate converts it into your currency) is never read as zero: the last value is carried over it, and the summary says how many days were missing. Days when the portfolio held nothing, before its first buy or after everything was sold, are not missing.

### What can make it change
Price moves, dividends, fees, currency effects and the selected period. A buy or a sale changes the money-weighted return; it changes the time-weighted return only through what the money does afterwards.

### Common misunderstanding
Neither figure is the gain over cost basis. Compare a benchmark with the time-weighted return, because a benchmark has no deposits or withdrawals either.

## Advanced metrics

### What it means
Five figures that describe the return and the risk of your portfolios taken together: **Annual yield**, **Volatility**, **Sharpe Ratio**, **Max Drawdown** and **Risk level**. Each has its own section below.

### Where it appears
The **Advanced metrics** section of the Portfolios page, on the web and in the mobile app. It covers all your portfolios together, or the portfolios of the members chosen in the member filter. A single portfolio's page does not have it.

### Which history it reads
- **Annual yield** reads every buy, sale and dividend you have recorded, whatever period is selected.
- On the web, **Volatility**, **Max Drawdown** and **Risk level** read the portfolio's daily history from the first day of the selected period. In the mobile app they read the whole history, and the period selector does not change them.
- **Sharpe Ratio** combines the annual yield with the volatility, so on the web it sets an all-time yield against the selected period's volatility.

### When a figure is missing
A figure that cannot be calculated shows a short reason in place of a number. It is never shown as zero.

- **Needs a year of history**: the annual yield, and the Sharpe ratio that depends on it, need at least 365 days between your first recorded transaction and today (or your last transaction, when you no longer hold anything).
- **Needs 3 months of price history** and **Needs 3 months of history to score risk**: volatility and the risk level need at least 90 daily values. On the web, a period shorter than that, such as 1 Month, shows this message however old the portfolio is.
- **Needs a longer drawdown window**: max drawdown needs at least 30 daily values.
- With between 1 and 29 days of history in what it reads, the section adds the note **Performance history needs about a month of daily snapshots.** With no history at all, the web adds no note and the mobile app says **Risk metrics need more price history.**

The figures are also left out, with the same messages, when they cannot be measured in your currency: a transaction with no exchange rate for its date leaves out the figures that read it, and a day of history that cannot be converted leaves out volatility, risk level and max drawdown. A holding with more units sold than bought leaves all five out until its transactions are corrected.

### Common misunderstanding
These figures describe the past. They are not a forecast or a rating, and WonderMoney does not use them to recommend anything.

## Annual yield

### What it means
The yearly rate your money has earned across all your portfolios since your first transaction. It is a money-weighted return: the same `XIRR` calculation described in [Time-weighted and money-weighted return](#time-weighted-and-money-weighted-return), run over the whole history and not over the selected period.

### How it is calculated
Every buy (with its fee) counts as money put in, every sale (net of its fee) and every dividend as money received, and what you hold today as a final amount received today. The annual yield is the rate `r` that balances them:

```text
sum of amount / (1 + r)^(days since the first transaction / 365) = 0
```

Amounts are converted into your currency at the exchange rate of each transaction's date.

### How to read it
A positive figure means your money grew at that rate per year; a negative one means it shrank. Money that was invested for longer weighs more.

### What can make it change
Price moves, new buys and sales, dividends, fees and exchange rates. The selected period does not change it.

### Common misunderstanding
It is not a dividend yield, and it is not the return of the selected period. It is not shown for a history shorter than a year, because stretching a few months into a year would exaggerate it.

## Volatility and risk level

### What it means
Volatility says how much the value of your portfolios moved from day to day, as a yearly percentage. **Risk level** is a label read from it.

### How it is calculated
1. For each pair of consecutive days in the history, the daily return is `(value today - money added today + money taken out today) / value yesterday - 1`. Buys and sales are taken out, so a deposit is not read as a gain or a sale as a loss.
2. Volatility is the standard deviation of those daily returns, multiplied by `sqrt(365)` to make it yearly. The history has one value per calendar day, weekends and holidays included, which is why the factor is 365 and not the 252 trading days often used elsewhere.
3. The risk level follows the volatility:

| Volatility | Risk level |
|---|---|
| Below 15% | **Low** |
| 15% to below 25% | **Medium** |
| 25% or more | **High** |

### How to read it
A higher figure means larger daily swings, up or down. Compare it only with a volatility measured the same way: a figure built on trading days alone is not directly comparable.

### What can make it change
Price moves, exchange rates, the mix of holdings and, on the web, the selected period.

### What it does not account for
- Days with a missing value on either side are skipped, so a gap in the history removes those days from the measure.
- Dividends you received are not part of the daily values.
- The risk level reads volatility alone. It does not look at concentration, the kind of assets you hold, or your own circumstances.

### Common misunderstanding
**Low** does not mean safe, and **High** does not mean a loss is coming. The label only says how much the value has moved in the history measured.

## Sharpe ratio

### What it means
The return earned above a risk-free rate for each unit of volatility.

### How it is calculated
`(annual yield - 4.5) / volatility`, with all three as yearly percentages. The risk-free rate is fixed at 4.5% per year, an approximate US Treasury rate. It is the same for every currency and does not follow market rates.

### How to read it
A higher ratio means more return for the swings taken. A negative ratio means the annual yield was below 4.5%.

### What can make it change
Anything that changes the annual yield or the volatility.

### What it does not account for
- It needs both a year of transactions and 90 daily values, and it is not shown when the volatility is zero.
- The fixed rate may be far from the rate available in your currency, which shifts the ratio up or down.
- On the web, the yield covers your whole history and the volatility the selected period, so the two halves can cover different stretches of time.

### Common misunderstanding
A Sharpe ratio is only comparable with another one that uses the same risk-free rate and the same way of annualising.

## Max drawdown

### What it means
The largest fall from a peak to a later low in the history measured, as a percentage.

### How it is calculated
An index starts at 100 and moves each day by the same daily return used for [volatility](#volatility-and-risk-level), so deposits and withdrawals do not move it. The drawdown on a day is `(highest index so far - index that day) / highest index so far`, and the max drawdown is the largest of them.

### How to read it
A max drawdown of 20% means that, at the worst point, the investments stood 20% below their earlier high. It says nothing about how long the fall lasted or whether it has been recovered. A figure of 0% means the index never closed below an earlier high.

### What can make it change
Price moves, exchange rates and, on the web, the selected period: a shorter period can miss an older, deeper fall.

### Common misunderstanding
It is not the loss on your cost basis, and it is not the most the portfolio could fall. It only reports the worst fall that happened in the history measured, from daily values, so moves within a day are not seen.

## Dividends and income

### What it means
Cash income paid by investments, usually from dividends or similar distributions.

### Where it appears
Transaction lists, income summaries, and performance views. The **Dividend Income** section adds up the dividend transactions in your portfolios, and the **Calendar** lists upcoming dividend dates; both are described below.

### How to read it
Treat it as part of total return when you want the full picture.

### What can make it change
New dividend payments, reinvestments, withholding taxes, and corrections.

### Dividend Income
The **Dividend Income** section is on the Portfolios page, for all your portfolios together, and on each portfolio's page, on the web and in the mobile app. It appears only when the portfolios hold at least one dividend transaction. The web and mobile apps cannot add a dividend at present, and a CSV import brings in buys and sells only (see [Add portfolio transactions](/en/docs/portfolio-investments/add-portfolio-transactions#supported-transaction-types)), so most portfolios do not show this section.

- **Total Dividends** is the sum of every dividend transaction in the portfolios, since the first one. It does not follow the selected period.
- **Dividends by Asset** splits that total by security, largest first.
- Amounts are in your currency. A dividend recorded in another currency is converted at the exchange rate of its date. One with no rate for that date is left out of the totals, and a note says how many were left out.

The section counts dividend transactions only, at their recorded amounts. It does not fetch the dividends a security paid, and it does not estimate future income.

### Upcoming dividends in the Calendar
The **Calendar** section is on the Portfolios page on the web. Under **Upcoming Dividends**, it lists the ticker and the ex-dividend date of dividends announced for the securities you hold, for the next 120 days, soonest first, up to ten entries. It follows the member filter.

The dates come from the server's market data provider. A dividend that has not been announced yet is not listed, no amount is shown, and nothing is added to your transactions when the date arrives. **Upcoming Earnings** has no data at present and shows **No upcoming events**.

### Common misunderstanding
Income is not the same as profit on price movement.

## Allocation and holding weight

### What it means
Allocation shows how the portfolio is spread across assets. Holding weight shows how much one holding contributes to the whole.

### Where it appears
Allocation charts, holding tables, and portfolio summaries.

### How to read it
Look for concentration, balance, and whether one holding dominates the portfolio.

### What can make it change
New buys and sales, price changes and exchange rates. Dividends, deposits and withdrawals do not change it: a portfolio holds no cash balance.

### Common misunderstanding
Allocation describes structure, not a recommendation.

## Benchmark comparison

### What it means
A benchmark is a reference used to compare portfolio performance.

### Where it appears
Performance benchmark views and return charts.

### How to read it
Compare the portfolio and benchmark over the same time range and with the same currency context.

### What can make it change
Portfolio returns, benchmark price moves, and the selected time range.

### Common misunderstanding
A benchmark is a comparison line, not a suggestion to copy.

## Currency effects

### What it means
The part of the result caused by exchange-rate movement when holdings use another currency.

### Where it appears
Performance and value views for multi-currency holdings.

### How to read it
Separate asset performance from currency movement when you want to understand the source of change.

### What can make it change
Exchange-rate changes and foreign-currency holdings.

### Common misunderstanding
Currency effect is not the same as the asset’s own price move.

## Fees

### What it means
Tracked costs paid to trade, hold, or manage investments.

### Where it appears
Transaction views, return summaries, and realized results.

### How to read it
Fees reduce your net result, so review them alongside gains and income.

### What can make it change
New transactions, broker charges, and corrected imports.

### Common misunderstanding
Fees are part of performance, not a separate topic.

## Time range

### What it means
The period used to calculate or display performance and comparison data.

### Where it appears
Performance charts, benchmark views, and summary cards.

### How to read it
Short ranges can be noisy; longer ranges often show the underlying trend more clearly.

### What can make it change
Changing the selected period or moving the chart window.

### Common misunderstanding
Different time ranges can produce very different return figures.
